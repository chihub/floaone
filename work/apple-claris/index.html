Apple / Claris · Design Manager · 2012 — 2019

A platform more than 30 years old, made coherent again.

A platform that had been accumulating for more than 30 years, rebuilt into one cloud ecosystem by one team. Eight years, six products from zero to one, sixteen countries, twelve plus languages. The same fragmentation that split the platform also lived inside its flagship tool, and fixing one meant fixing the other.

The throughline

By the time I arrived, Claris FileMaker had been accumulating for more than 30 years. A set of products had grown up beside each other without ever being designed as one system, and the flagship tool inside that set had buried its most important features under decades of menus. Same disease, two scales. The platform had fragmented across its products; the tool had fragmented across its own interface. The reflex at either scale is to fix the surfaces. The actual work was to find where the thing had come apart and design the connections back, first across the ecosystem, then one level down, inside the tool at its center.

The case study

Building one ecosystem from many parts

The problem

Thirty years of building had left a set of products that had never been designed as one. An app development IDE with a drag and drop builder and an embedded scripting engine, a path for importing data into custom tables, an on premises hosting server with its own monitoring console, and the clients that ran finished apps on desktop, web, and iOS. Each had grown on its own schedule to its own logic. A loyal global community of agencies, IT professionals, and business specialists built real work on top of it, for government, education, healthcare, and manufacturing, so the platform was proven. It was capable. It was just not one thing, and not modern. What made the problem unusual was the agreement underneath it: the customers had already said exactly where it had to go, a data and business workflow platform that lived on the cloud.

Image

We have a tight customer community. We know who they are and what they want to achieve, so the question was never what to build. It was how to turn these various product ideas into one ecosystem.

The response

Start by deciding what the whole platform was for, before deciding what any one product would do. The team built a short and long term journey map from a four stage research study, and it resolved into a single north star across six capability areas: development, hosting, licensing, data connection, scripting, and web integration. That structure is what kept the product tracks pulling toward the same shape while every one of them was being rebuilt. From there the transformation moved on three fronts at once. Move deployment, licensing, and automation onto the cloud with modular, low code tools. Rebuild the clients that ran the finished apps on web and mobile. And carry Apple's design principles into the Claris brand so the ecosystem read as one thing, not four.

Image

How the team worked

Six products rebuilt at once by one team is a recipe for six different products. What kept them one was that every product ran the same path, and the path was a loop, not a line. Each effort moved through the same eleven stages, from a research deck and user journey, through system flowchart, visual concepts, and low fidelity wireframes, into usability testing, design iteration, development and design systems, UX writing, design QA, and downstream. The point was not the sequence. It was that feedback and success criteria fed back into the work instead of waiting at the end, so usability testing changed the design while there was still time to change it. Under an eight year delivery schedule across six products, the reflex is to straighten the loop into a checklist and ship. Holding the loop open is what let iteration survive that pressure here, and it is why the products that shipped were the ones research had actually changed.

The path also defined what a designer owned. With one owner and one handoff at every stage, designers spent their judgment on the design decision in front of them, not on relitigating where the work was supposed to go next. The Macadamian research became the journey map. The journey map became the system flowchart for the commerce, admin, and cloud consoles. The flowchart became Customer Console iterations, and those resolved into Legato templates the whole community could build on. Each artifact was the input to the next, which is how a small team produced a coherent ecosystem instead of six disconnected efforts.

Image

The outcome

Six products, all built from zero to one, across eight years. Three were the cloud itself: FileMaker Cloud, the AWS console for hosting, software download, dashboards, and user management; Claris Connect, the platform that automated workflows across systems; and the Storefront for license and seat management. One was the service underneath them, FileMaker Server, the administration tool that ran the hosting. And two were the clients that put finished apps in people's hands: WebDirect 2.0 on the web, and FileMaker Go on mobile, which reached more than 4M App Store downloads in 2017. The desktop IDE was rebuilt incrementally alongside all of it. Underneath the launches sits the part that lasted: one cross platform design language across desktop, web, and iOS, carried into 16 countries and 12+ languages, and a design function that held a three front transformation together without losing the thread. The products are what the team shipped. The system is what let them keep shipping.

Inside the work

Rebuilding the tool at the center

The ecosystem was the leadership problem. The flagship tool was the same problem one level down. FileMaker Pro sat at the center of everything the team was building, and it carried the platform's whole history as a learning curve: an unclear starting point, scattered controls, critical features buried in menus. Research turned the frustration into one blunt finding. People could not get started. One user never made headway importing a table; another never used the program enough to find out.

We already knew the path the work actually followed, select a data connection, import data, model the data and its logic, build the interface, then debug and publish. Five steps, in order, every time. The tool was organized around its own internals instead of that path, so the fix was the same one we ran on the ecosystem: stop arranging things by how the software was built, and arrange them by how people actually move through the work. The redesign regrouped scattered controls into stages that matched the mental model, and carried the same logic from the desktop to a lightweight builder on the web. None of it added capability the product lacked. It surfaced what decades of menus had buried, in the order people expected to find it.

Understanding what users think was not the step before the design. It was the design.

One discipline, two scales. The platform fragmented across its products and the tool fragmented across its own menus were the same failure, and both answered to the same move: find where the work has come apart, then build the structure that holds it together. Doing that across an ecosystem is a leadership job; doing it inside a single tool is the proof the instinct holds all the way down. That is what eight years at Claris were for.
