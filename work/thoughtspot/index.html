ThoughtSpot · Director of Platform Design · 2020 — 2022

A system built to outlast the person who built it.

A globally distributed product organization with no shared design language, building the same things twice. The teams could design; nothing connected their decisions. Rather than police it, I led a platform design function that teams chose to use: a component library, a design-to-code toolchain, and a governance model that earned its authority by being useful first.

The case study

Building the connective tissue

The problem

The same patterns got rebuilt in more than one place, and no two versions behaved alike. That was the visible symptom. Underneath it, every feature team designed its own surface on its own logic, with nothing connecting those decisions. Patterns duplicated across the product. Specs drifted, so components meant to match shipped inconsistent. And the real cost landed on the people doing the work: designers spent their days reconciling which version was right instead of making anything new. There was no platform design function. There was no one whose job was the system.

Image

The product was fragmenting faster than any single team could fix it, because no single team owned the connections between them.

The response

It started with a vision deck. That deck supported three product launches in 2021, and out of it came a design systems roadmap that the product teams across the organization actually followed. The order matters: the function earned a mandate by being useful on real launches first, then turned that credibility into a system everyone built from. The platform design team was positioned as a service to Product and Engineering, not a gatekeeper over them. That stance decided every structural choice that followed. From there the work moved along one connected pipeline, from a systems vision deck through architectural UI standards, into a functional component library, into developer tools and design QA, and out to delivery, mapped the whole way against the product development cycle. The first act was analyzing the patterns already repeating across the product, stepping back from individual features to ask what the product was doing across all of them, then abstracting the answer into reusable interaction models. Finding the pattern is an organizational act, not a design act. It required access to every team's work and the trust of every team's designer.

What the team built

A shared foundation across the breadth of the product: architecture, navigation, color, typography, user input, data viewer, and help system, consolidated into one component library where there had been none. Then the half of the system most design systems skip. A Figma library that only designers use is not a design system. It is a design archive. So the build extended into engineering's own workflow: a component library in Storybook, CSS handoff, design tokens, Storybook control knobs, interactive prototypes, and shared grid systems. Those are engineering tools. Building them was a decision to meet engineering where they work rather than ask them to adapt to a designer's process. Around the artifacts came the documentation portal, the single place a feature team could go to understand a pattern without asking a designer first: functional models, workflow patterns, component types, interaction behaviors, usage guidelines, specifications, and a decision matrix for when to reach for what.

How the team worked

A connective-tissue team: a small, specialist platform group, roughly five people across designers, UI engineers, and a UX writer, embedded in a distributed organization and sitting at the intersection of Product, Engineering, and Design. It owned four core activities: the systems design roadmap, the systems documentation, the designer and developer tools, and the cross-team relationships. And it provided something different to each function it touched. Product got feature UX analysis, design systems showcases, and alignment. Engineering got prototypes, CSS handoff, design tokens, configuration specs, and debugging support. Design got feature UX review, handoff QA, pattern analysis, and a path for new component requests. The scoping was the design. A team like this that tries to do every team's design work stops connecting and starts competing. Keeping a five-person team to its defined interfaces is what let it serve a whole distributed org without being absorbed into any one part of it.

The outcome

Three numbers tell the adoption story, and adoption is the only thing that matters for a system like this. The Figma library reached 12K activities a week. The developer portal held 140+ components, used by the entire engineering group. The documentation site became the primary design reference for 8+ feature teams. What counts is that engineering used the system by choice. And none of it was a one-time build. It was sustained by a governance model that ran on usefulness instead of authority: cross-product analysis to find alignment, a quarterly roadmap to set shared direction, recurring show and tell for visibility, steady documentation releases to cut ambiguity, office hours to build trust, and a newsletter to keep awareness up. None of those mechanisms needed authority to function. They needed to be worth a team's time. The clearest proof is the simplest one. The system kept being used after I left.

Close

A design system is easy to mistake for its component library. The system is the set of reasons a distributed organization keeps building the same way after no one is forcing it to. Build those reasons, and the library takes care of itself.
