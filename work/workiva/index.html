Workiva · Senior UX Manager · 2022 — Present

Breaking the formless into things a team can build.

A platform serving over 75% of the Fortune 500, a reporting lifecycle that has to serve three solutions at once, a modernization effort stalled for a decade, a design function stuck downstream. I lead a design team embedded across 10+ agile teams and 150+ engineers, and the work comes down to two jobs: get design into the decisions about what gets built, and make a distributed team able to build them. The move is the same in both: take something formless and break it into named parts people can act on.

The case study

Two jobs: the roadmap and the teams

The problem

A pattern kept repeating. By the time a problem reached design, the decisions that shaped it had already been made somewhere else. On a platform this size, the work that matters most sits in the gaps between teams, each seeing only its own part. The decade-old platform, the unowned lifecycle, the stalled modernization: different problems, one condition. Nobody held both the view of the whole and the standing to act on it.

The important things belonged to everyone, which is to say no one. That is exactly why they stayed broken.

What I led

The roadmap

Earning the seat

When I arrived, design was downstream. Product set the roadmap, engineering scoped it, and design was handed the result to make usable. Arguing for a seat does not earn one. Usefulness does. The instrument was the UX Project Sizing Framework, which I authored. It reframes the first question a team asks, from how long will this take to what level of complexity will shape what we deliver, and that question can only be asked while the roadmap is being made. Used steadily, it did what arguing never could: designers moved from making the roadmap usable to helping decide what it should be, in planning, in customer insight synthesis, and through development and release. The seat was earned one useful contribution at a time.

Spending it: modernization

The first thing the seat was spent on was a problem that had defeated the organization for a decade: the core experiences, the oldest part of the platform, which no single team owned. Teams owned pieces, so anyone could add and no one could fix. Every prior attempt had died the same way, and I named why: no definition, no dedicated team, no objective everyone had to answer to, and a habit of cosmetic changes standing in for structural work, fresh paint on a car that needed an engine. Instead of another strategy document, I helped form a small core team across product, engineering, program, and design, and secured what every prior attempt had lacked: an objective set at the top, which gave other teams permission to spend time on it.

We ran the build in reverse, a code-to-design exercise: architectural principles first, a working prototype in three weeks, then iteration on the real thing instead of debate over mockups. I was flexible on design opinions and immovable on one principle: a clear boundary between the platform's foundation and the areas other teams controlled, so the platform set the strategy instead of the verticals dictating it. The politics were handled as deliberately: a closed room, decisions made by the few people accountable, one sponsor carrying them upward. Modernization moved for the first time in ten years.

Scaling it: the reporting lifecycle

The same seat shaped the larger question: how one platform serves three product lines. A regulated report runs a defined lifecycle, seven stages from planning and gathering data through building, review, filing, and measuring what to improve. UX leadership defines that lifecycle, and my platform team turned it into the capabilities the stages run on, built once and inherited by Financial Reporting, GRC, and Sustainability. There are four capability areas: data, the document layer governed by regulatory requirements, collaboration where review and signoff happen, and process management, which ties the other three together across entities and regions.

My role differed by layer, and the honest version is the more useful one. Connective on data, where other teams owned the inbound pieces. Collaborative on document and collaboration, defining the modules with engineering and product. Directional on process management, where I framed the problem and set the focus. The payoff sits in the last two. Reporting had always been sequential, one person finishing before the next could start. Real-time collaboration and parallel process management turned that bottleneck into parallel workstreams, which is what a correct decomposition buys.

Image

Directing it: the AI

The newest roadmap question was the least formed: how AI enters a product where one inconsistent figure is a regulatory finding. I did not direct that build decision by decision. I built the frame that made the decisions. One idea, an assembly line, not a chatbot: specialized agents, each with one job, handing off down the line, governance set once and inherited, every claim traceable to a source, a gap flagged rather than guessed at. Once the team aligned on that frame, the agent structure, the one-job-per-stage rule, and the inherited compliance floor were no longer open arguments. The workflow now runs inside the process layer my team owns. The leadership contribution was not any single agent. It was the frame that made the whole team build the same thing.

What I led

The teams

One function, not scattered outposts

A design team, embedded across more than ten agile squads alongside 150+ engineers. The risk in that shape is fragmentation along the same lines as the org chart. The sizing framework did double duty: the tool that opened the roadmap door also gave every designer one shared planning language, so the function scoped work the same way in every squad. The Customer Value Roadmap made the function's contribution legible to the business, and individual growth plans tied each designer's daily work to where they were trying to go. The measure of whether it worked came from outside: product managers across the pillars described the designers as the ones who pulled teams together.

Aligning by simplifying

The working method under everything is reduction. Most alignment fails because the picture is too complicated to agree on: people nod at a complex plan, then build different things because each understood a different part. So I simplify the story until a team can hold all of it: a few experience goals and one visual flow, captured in a collective PRD built with the team rather than handed to it, which then defines the components to build. The artifacts are low fidelity on purpose, because a polished mockup invites argument about pixels before the goals are settled. They exist to settle the expensive decisions first, so by the time the team executes, execution is fast because it is no longer also a negotiation.

The outcome

The roadmap changed, and so did design's place in it. Strategy came to want design in the room because the team kept being useful.
