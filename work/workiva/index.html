Workiva · Senior UX Manager · 2022 — Present

Breaking the formless into things a team can build.

A platform serving over 75% of the Fortune 500, a reporting lifecycle that has to serve three solutions at once, a modernization effort stalled for a decade, a design function stuck downstream. I lead a design team embedded across 10+ agile teams and 150+ engineers, and the work comes down to two jobs: get design into the decisions about what gets built, and make a distributed team able to build them. The move is the same in both: take something formless and break it into named parts people can act on.

The case study

Two jobs: the roadmap and the teams

The problem

A pattern kept repeating. By the time a problem reached design, the decisions that shaped it had already been made somewhere else. That is what a platform this size tends to produce: the work that matters most sits in the gaps between teams, each seeing only its own part. The evidence was everywhere. A platform serving most of the Fortune 500, grown for a decade by stacking new experiences on old foundations. A reporting lifecycle spanning three product lines that no single team owned end to end. A modernization effort that had stalled for ten years. A design function handed decisions to make usable after they were already made. Different problems, one condition: nobody held both the view of the whole and the standing to act on it.

The important things belonged to everyone, which is to say no one. That is exactly why they stayed broken.

What I led

The roadmap

Earning the seat

When I arrived, design was downstream. Product set the roadmap, engineering scoped it, and design was handed the result to make usable. Arguing for a seat does not earn one. Usefulness does. The instrument was the UX Project Sizing Framework, which I authored: on its surface a planning tool, in practice a way into the roadmap conversation, because it reframes the first question a team asks, from how long will this take to what level of complexity will shape what we deliver. That question cannot be answered after the roadmap is set. It has to be asked while the roadmap is being made, which is exactly where design had not been. Used steadily, the framework did what arguing never could. Designers moved from making the roadmap usable to helping decide what it should be, in roadmap planning, in customer insight synthesis, in the partnership through development and release. The seat was earned one useful contribution at a time, until leaving design out of the planning felt like a mistake the partners did not want to make.

Spending it: modernization

The first thing the seat was spent on was the problem that had defeated the organization for a decade. The core experiences were the oldest part of the platform, modern work layered over a legacy foundation so the seams showed, and underneath sat the fact that made it intractable: the core experience had no single owner. Teams owned pieces, so anyone could add and no one could fix. Every prior attempt had died the same way, and I named why. Not as a complaint, as a diagnosis: no definition, no dedicated team, no objective everyone had to answer to, and a long habit of cosmetic changes standing in for structural work, fresh paint on a car that needed an engine. The diagnosis turned a feeling into nameable problems. Then, instead of another strategy document, I helped form a small dedicated core team across product, engineering, program, and design, and went after the one thing every prior attempt had lacked: an organizational objective set at the top, the permission other teams needed to spend time on it.

We ran the build in reverse, a code to design exercise. The small group set the architectural principles, had a working prototype in three weeks, and iterated on the real thing instead of debating mockups. I held the principles and stayed out of the pixels early. On design opinions I was flexible. On principle, immovable: a clear boundary between the platform's foundation and the areas other teams controlled, so the platform set the strategy instead of the verticals dictating it. The politics were handled just as deliberately, a closed room, collective decisions among the few people accountable, one sponsor carrying the selling upward. Modernization moved for the first time in ten years.

Scaling it: the reporting lifecycle

The same seat shaped the larger roadmap question: how one platform serves three product lines. Producing a regulated report runs a defined lifecycle, seven stages from planning and gathering data through building the report, review, filing, and measuring what to improve. UX leadership defines that lifecycle. My platform team turned the definition into the capabilities the stages run on, built once and inherited by Financial Reporting, GRC, and Sustainability rather than rebuilt by each. Four capability areas: the data layer, the document layer governed by regulatory requirements, the collaboration layer where review and signoff happen, and the process management layer that ties the other three together and carries the work across entities and regions. My role was not uniform across them, and the honest version is the more useful one. Connective on data, where other teams owned the inbound pieces and the work was linking the layer into the larger system. Collaborative on document and collaboration, defining the modules with engineering and product. Directional on process management, where I framed the problem space and set the focus, the decision that shapes everything downstream. The concrete payoff sits in the last two layers. Reporting had always been sequential, one person finishing before the next could start. Real-time collaboration and parallel process management turned that bottleneck into parallel team workstreams. The pieces became independent enough that people could work them at the same time, which is what a correct decomposition buys.

Image

Directing it: the AI

The newest roadmap question was the least formed: how AI enters a product where one inconsistent figure is a regulatory finding. I did not direct that build decision by decision. I built the frame that made the decisions. One idea, an assembly line, not a chatbot: a sequence of specialized agents, each with one job, handing off down the line, governance set once and inherited, every claim traceable to a source, a gap flagged rather than guessed at. I put the frame into an artifact the whole team could align to, and it settled a hundred downstream questions before they became debates. Once the team accepted the assembly line, the agent structure, the one job per stage rule, and the inherited compliance floor were no longer open arguments. The frame had already decided them. The workflow now runs inside the process layer my team owns: gathering the regulatory requirements, assigning the work to the right teams, collecting and modeling the data, checking the health of the report, then auto-generating the draft and rolling data forward against the next reporting timeline. The leadership contribution was not any single agent. It was the frame that made the whole team build the same thing.

What I led

The teams

One function, not scattered outposts

A design team, embedded across more than ten agile squads alongside 150+ engineers, spread over multiple product pillars. The risk in that shape is fragmentation along the same lines the org chart already fragments. The sizing framework did double duty here. The same tool that opened the roadmap door gave every designer, in every context, one shared planning language, so the function scoped work the same way no matter which squad it sat in. Alongside it, the Customer Value Roadmap, a consensus artifact that connected several teams and made the function's contribution legible to the business, the thing leadership could finally see.

Aligning by simplifying

The working method underneath everything is reduction. Most alignment fails because the picture is too complicated to agree on. People nod at a complex plan and then build different things, because they each understood a different part of it. So I simplify the story until a team can hold the whole of it in their head: the few experience goals and the single visual flow that decide everything else, captured in a collective PRD built with the team rather than handed to it. From the goals and the flow, the PRD defines the components the team needs to build. The artifacts are low fidelity on purpose. A polished mockup invites argument about pixels, fonts, and spacing, exactly the wrong conversation before the goals are settled. A rough one keeps the debate where it belongs. We do not make these artifacts to execute from. We make them to sharpen focus, settle the decisions that are expensive to get wrong, and only then let the building begin. By the time the team executes, the hard calls are already made and shared, so execution is fast because it is no longer also a negotiation.

Growing the people

Individual growth plans tied each designer's daily work to where they were trying to go. Shared collaborative spaces kept visibility across distributed triads cheap, so staying aligned cost little. The measure of whether any of it worked was not my own assessment. It was the product managers across the pillars describing the designers as instrumental, the ones who pulled teams together, the rare talent the organization was surprised to find it had.

The outcome

The roadmap changed, and so did design's place in it. Modernization moved for the first time in a decade: the objective secured from the top, a prototype built in three weeks carried to working code, executive buy-in, other teams building into the new standard rather than around it. The lifecycle became four reusable capability areas serving three solutions, with reporting turned from a sequential bottleneck into parallel team workstreams and the agentic workflow running inside the process layer end to end. And the function itself moved from inheriting the roadmap to helping set it, a sizing framework shared past its own walls to operations leadership, a roadmap artifact adopted as the model for how design communicates value, designers with the standing to represent UX inside engineering and product circles. The seat was not granted. It was earned by the team being useful enough that strategy wanted it there.
