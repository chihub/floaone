Practice

How the work gets made.

Six practices, built over 20+ years of leading teams through real complexity, each one an answer to a specific way that design work breaks at scale. None of it is theory. Every practice here started as a failure I watched happen and then built a response to, so it would not happen again. They are in order on purpose. Each one answers the question the last one leaves open, working out from the single design decision to how the whole system ships.

01

Principles

Next: Systems Thinking ↓

Six beliefs, formed by leading real teams through real problems. Each one is the lesson left behind by a specific failure.

01

Know your audience

The failure this answers is designing for an assumption. When you build for who you imagine the user is, you build for yourself. The discipline is to keep finding out who they actually are, before the work starts and while it continues.

02

Have a customer-first strategy

The failure is optimizing for the roadmap instead of the person it is meant to serve. Features get shipped, metrics move, and the customer's actual problem stays where it was. Strategy that does not start with the customer is just a list of things to build.

03

Solve problems with a system

The failure is the one-off fix that works once and scales to nothing. A solution that lives in a single screen has to be re-solved on the next screen, and the one after that. Build the system, and the answer holds across everything downstream of it.

04

Focus on value over activities

The failure is mistaking motion for progress. Teams stay busy, ship steadily, and produce output that no one can connect to an outcome. The question that cuts through it is simple: what changed because we did this?

05

Uncover operational blind spots

The failure is designing for the visible problem while the real one compounds out of sight. The loudest issue is rarely the most expensive one. The work is to find what no one is looking at, because that is usually where the cost is accumulating.

06

Create a purpose

The failure is a team that executes without knowing why. People do their best work when the work means something, and a leader's job is to make the reason legible, not assumed.

These are not aspirations. They are the operating instructions I have arrived at, and the five sections that follow are what they look like applied to the product, to engineering, to the team, to governance, and to the way the whole thing ships.

02

Systems Thinking

Next: Design-to-Code ↓
The failure

A product that fragments faster than any single team can fix it. In a distributed organization, fragmentation is not a sign that teams are designing badly. It is a sign that nothing is connecting their decisions. Feature teams each define their own use cases, patterns drift apart, handoffs break, and inconsistency accumulates faster than anyone can clean it up.

The response

Step back from individual features and ask what the product is doing across all of them. At ThoughtSpot, that meant finding the repeating patterns, abstracting them into reusable interaction models, and embedding them into a pipeline that ran from systems vision through to shipped product. At Apple / Claris the same instinct ran one level higher: a four-stage research study became a journey map, and the journey map resolved into a north star across six capability areas that decided what the platform was for before product decided what each piece would do. One reads the patterns inside a product; the other defines the shape the whole product line is built toward. The reflex is to fix the surfaces. The actual work is to design the connections between them.

Image
The outcome

Patterns shared instead of rebuilt, and a product that holds together as it grows. Seeing across a whole product line takes access to every team's work, the trust of every team's designer, and the standing to say this one should be shared. That is the line between a design leader and a designer.

03

Design-to-Code

Next: Team Operations ↓
The failure

A design system that everyone admires and no one uses. Design systems break at the handoff. A Figma library that only designers open is not a system, it is an archive. The gap between design and engineering is not a tooling gap. It is a trust-and-infrastructure gap, and tools are only evidence that it has been closed.

The response

Build the whole chain across the gap. At ThoughtSpot, that was a component library in Storybook, the engineering tools around it, and a documentation portal feature teams adopted as their primary reference, each piece built around how engineers already work, not how designers wish they would.

Image
The outcome

Adoption, not availability: engineering used the system by choice, because it was built into engineering's own workflow.

04

Team Operations

Next: Design Governance ↓
The failure

A team that delivers in pieces because it was never built to deliver as one. Building the team is a design decision, and when it is left unmade, the work fragments along the same lines the org chart does.

The response

Design the team with the same care as the product, and fit the model to the moment. At Apple / Claris, that was an end-to-end function with clear ownership at every stage, from research through QA and explicit downstream accountability, built over eight years. At ThoughtSpot, it was the opposite shape: a smaller specialist team embedded in a distributed organization, with five clearly scoped responsibilities, brand and experience standards, the design and handoff workflow, the toolkits, the cross-team delivery chain, and the documentation. Not a full-service design team. Connective tissue between the teams that already existed.

Image
The outcome

Two organizations, two structures, the same result: a team that stayed consistent across complexity. Both models answer one question in different ways, and the right answer depends on the organization, the stage of the product, and the kind of leadership the moment is asking for. Reading the moment correctly is the skill.

05

Design Governance

Next: Delivery Framework ↓
The failure

Quality treated as a talent problem when it is really a structural one. Governance fails the moment it is felt as oversight, a gate that slows teams down and gets in the way without adding value.

The response

Build governance that teams reach for because it makes their own work easier, useful before it is authoritative. At ThoughtSpot, that meant mechanisms that ran on usefulness instead of mandate: shared roadmaps to set direction, recurring forums to build visibility, office hours to keep trust alive. None of them needed authority to function. They needed to be worth using.

Image
The outcome

A system that earns its use, which means it does not have to be enforced. That is the leadership model under everything on this page: trust is built by helping people do their job better.

06

Delivery Framework

Back to top ↑
The failure

Upstream thinking that never reliably reaches downstream execution. Strategy without delivery is just documentation.

The response

A mechanism that turns design decisions into shipped product consistently, across teams and over time, in three layers. The first is documentation that stays accurate enough to reduce ambiguity at every handoff, from functional models to dimensional specs. The second is architecture: the layout system, navigation model, and workflow patterns that decide how users move through the product, holding no matter which team built which screen. The third is the pipeline itself, a stage-gate model that carries work from systems vision to shipped product without losing intent. Every stage has one clear output and one clear consumer.

Image
The outcome

Quality built in along the way rather than inspected at the end, and a closed loop. The principles set the beliefs, systems thinking applies them to the product, the design-to-code chain carries them to engineering, the team and its governance hold them steady, and the delivery framework ships them. One connected system, which is the whole argument of this page.
